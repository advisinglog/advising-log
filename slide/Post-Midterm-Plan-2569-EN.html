How to run a test session (~30 min per user)

Testing with real users: one session, step by step

Before

Prepare

  • Recruit users from your real target group, not classmates
  • Get informed consent (PDPA) and ask before recording audio or video
  • Use codes U01, U02… instead of names
  • Prepare tasks, questionnaires, and an Excel sheet (one row per user)
  • Run one pilot first
During

Observe, don't help

  • Split roles: a Moderator (talks and gives tasks) and a Note-taker (times and records)
  • Tell the user "we are testing the system, not you"
  • Ask them to think aloud
  • Give tasks one at a time and never help or point
  • Record success, time, where they got stuck, and key quotes
After

Measure right away

  • If you use the SUS, give it immediately after the tasks
  • If you use TAM or UTAUT2, give it after the SUS
  • A 3-question interview: what did you like, where did you get stuck, would you keep using it and why
  • Thank the user and enter results into the sheet the same day
Intro + consent 3'→ Old way (baseline) 5'→ Tasks + think-aloud 10–15'→ Questionnaires (if used) 3–6'→ Interview 5'
Tip About 5 users per round usually uncover most of the major usability problems. If you can, test 5 → fix → test 5 more: you reach ≥ 10 sessions and get before/after-fix evidence too. (Nielsen, 2000)

Task-based testing

Tasks: what users try to do

How to write tasks
  • 3–5 tasks drawn from your locked core workflow (MUST)
  • Write them as scenarios, not button instructions
  • Each task has a clear success criterion
  • Order from easy to hard and use the same set for everyone
  • Measure: success, time on task, and errors
  • Have users do it the old way first (e.g., on paper) to get the before time
Example: CupLog
  • T1 "You just sold one latte. Record it."
    Success = correct item logged in ≤ 3 taps
  • T2 "The owner asks how many cups you sold today."
    Success = correct number given
  • T3 "You logged an Americano by mistake. Fix it."
    Success = entry corrected to latte
The trap Wrong: "Tap + and choose Latte." (You gave away the answer, so the result means nothing.)
Right: "You just sold one latte. Record it." (The user has to find the way.)

Measurement: required and optional

What is required and what is optional

Required: Tasks (Performance)

"Does it actually work?"

Success rate, time per task, errors, and before / after time. This is your evidence for P4 Impact.

Optional: SUS (Usability)

"How easy is it to use?"

A 0–100 score per user, averaged and compared with the benchmark of 68.

Optional: TAM / UTAUT2 (Acceptance)

"Will they adopt it?"

Mean score per construct, such as usefulness, ease of use, and intention to keep using.

How to choose Use SUS, TAM or UTAUT2: one, several, or none (pick only one of TAM and UTAUT2).
Combining them gives a clearer picture: a system can be easy to use (high SUS) while users see no benefit (low PU), so they won't keep using it.
If you use none of them, you must present other evidence; see the next slide.

No SUS, TAM or UTAUT2? Then show your evidence

Not using a standard instrument: what evidence to present

1 Method (What)

Which method you used

Name your method, e.g., task-based observation, post-use interviews, a multi-day field trial with usage logs, or a questionnaire your team wrote (attach the questions), and why you chose it.

2 Procedure (How)

How you ran it

Who, how many people, how many sessions, when and where; what users did, what you measured, and who in your team did what.

3 Results

What you found

Measured numbers such as success rate, before / after time, or number of uses; problems found with severity, user quotes, and what your team fixed.

4 Evidence

Traceable proof

Consent forms, photos or video, the raw data sheet, interview notes, or usage logs that the instructor can verify.

The bar Whatever method you choose, you must test with real users and be able to say whether the system really helps them, and how you know.
Vague claims such as "users said it was good", with no method and no evidence, do not count as test results.

Optional: System Usability Scale (Brooke, 1996)

SUS: 10 items, a 0–100 score

10 items, Likert 1–5 (strongly disagree → strongly agree)
  1. I think that I would like to use this system frequently.
  2. I found the system unnecessarily complex.
  3. I thought the system was easy to use.
  4. I think that I would need the support of a technical person to be able to use this system.
  5. I found the various functions in this system were well integrated.
  6. I thought there was too much inconsistency in this system.
  7. I would imagine that most people would learn to use this system very quickly.
  8. I found the system very cumbersome to use.
  9. I felt very confident using the system.
  10. I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going with this system.
Scoring
  • Odd items (1, 3, 5, 7, 9): score − 1
  • Even items (2, 4, 6, 8, 10): 5 − score
  • Sum all 10 × 2.5 → 0–100 per user
  • Report the mean and SD across users
Interpretation (Sauro & Lewis)
  • ≥ 80.8 = A (excellent)
  • 68 = average (C)
  • < 51.7 = F (needs fixing)
Watch out SUS is not a percentage (78 ≠ 78%)
don't drop or reword items (you may replace "system" with your product name)
use the overall score, not item-by-item
about 12–14 users give fairly stable results (Tullis & Stetson, 2004)

Optional: technology acceptance (if used, pick one model)

TAM or UTAUT2: will users accept it?

TAM (Davis, 1989): fits workplace tools
  • PU Perceived Usefulness
    "Using [system] helps me do ___ faster."
  • PEOU Perceived Ease of Use
    "Learning to use [system] is easy for me."
  • BI Behavioral Intention
    "I intend to keep using [system]."
  • 3–4 items per construct
UTAUT2 (Venkatesh, Thong & Xu, 2012): fits consumer apps
  • PE Performance Expectancy, EE Effort Expectancy
  • SI Social Influence, FC Facilitating Conditions
  • HM Hedonic Motivation, PV Price Value, HT Habit
  • All point to BI (intention to use)
  • Pick only the constructs that fit your system
  • HT can't be measured in a single session; use PV only for paid products
Rigor If you use one, choose one model and justify it in your Test Plan
use a 5-point Likert scale
with ~10–15 users, report only the mean ± SD per construct
Don't claim causal paths such as "PU drives BI": that needs SEM/PLS and a much larger sample.

Model: TAM (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh & Davis, 1996)

Technology Acceptance Model

Measured in this testNot measured (outside the scope of a single test session)
PEOU → PU

A system that is easy to use makes its usefulness easier to see.

PU → BI

When users see that the system really helps their work, they intend to keep using it. In many studies this is the strongest path.

PEOU → BI

Ease of use affects intention both directly and through PU.

Model: UTAUT2 (Venkatesh, Thong & Xu, 2012)

Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology 2

Measurable in this testMeasure when relevant (PV only for paid products; moderators collected as participant background)Cannot be measured in a single session
Reading the model All seven constructs point to BI. FC and HT also affect actual use (Use Behavior) directly. Age, gender and experience make these relationships stronger or weaker for different groups.

Optional: how to use the model in your project

Using a model in your project: 7 steps

1

Pick the model that fits the context

A workplace system that people are required to use calls for TAM. A consumer app that people choose for themselves calls for UTAUT2. Write your reason in the Test Plan.

2

Choose the constructs to measure

For TAM, measure all three: PU, PEOU, BI. For UTAUT2, pick the constructs that fit your system and always include BI. Leave out HT and Use Behavior.

3

Build items from the source papers

Adapt the items in the original papers, 3–4 per construct, changing only the system and task names. Don't write new items from scratch.

4

Translate and check meaning

Translate into Thai, have someone else translate it back into English (back-translation), and compare the meaning. Then pilot it with 2–3 people.

5

Collect the data

Give the questionnaire after the tasks and the SUS, on a 5-point Likert scale. With UTAUT2, also record age, gender, and experience.

6

Score it

Each user's construct score = the mean of that construct's items. Then report the mean and SD across users.

7

Interpret and fix

Link the lowest-scoring construct to problems you observed in the tasks, then fix the system. Don't claim causal relationships.

Writing questionnaire items

How to write good questions

Rules
  • Start from validated items in the source papers; change only the system and task names
  • One idea per item: never ask two things at once
  • Name the system and task clearly, e.g., "record sales", not "do my work"
  • No leading wording such as "this excellent system"
  • Avoid negative and double-negative statements
  • Use the same scale for every item: 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree
  • Give every item a code such as PU1, PU2, PU3 for analysis
Examples: wrong and right
  • Wrong: "CupLog is easy and fast." (two ideas)
    Right: split into "CupLog is easy to use." (PEOU) and "Using CupLog lets me record sales more quickly." (PU)
  • Wrong: "Don't you like this system that makes work easier?" (leading)
    Right: "I find CupLog useful in my work at the café."
  • Wrong: "I don't think CupLog is hard to use." (double negative)
    Right: "CupLog is easy to use."
  • Wrong: "This system is good." (too broad; which construct?)
    Right: word each item to match the construct it measures

Excel formulas (one row per user) 
' PU score for the user in row 2 (PU1–PU3 in B:D)
=AVERAGE(B2:D2)

' Mean and SD across users (PU scores in column E)
=AVERAGE(E2:E11)
=STDEV.S(E2:E11)

' SUS for the user in row 2 (items 1–10 in B:K)
=2.5*((B2-1)+(5-C2)+(D2-1)+(5-E2)+(F2-1)
     +(5-G2)+(H2-1)+(5-I2)+(J2-1)+(5-K2))
